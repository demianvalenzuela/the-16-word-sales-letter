# Chapter 5: In a World Full of Bullshit, Proof is King

Source: book, pp. 39-47. Q3: "How do I know this is real?"

## Core idea

An attractive new promise creates skepticism as well as interest. Show relevant proof early and arrange it into a causal narrative the reader can follow.

## Frameworks introduced

### ABT: And, But, Therefore

Evaldo draws on Randy Olson's account of Trey Parker's narrative rule. Use it when a proof section is a sequence of disconnected facts.

1. **And:** establish the situation and the conditions needed to understand the result.
2. **But:** introduce the obstacle, contradiction, or unexpected development.
3. **Therefore:** show the resulting action or outcome and its implication for the argument.

The connective words need not appear literally. The relationship between events matters more than a verbal formula. A result that does not follow from the preceding facts is not repaired by adding "therefore."

Why it helps: conflict and consequence give the reader a reason to process evidence. Failure mode: using a good story to disguise weak evidence or to imply that an exceptional result is typical.

### Initial proof versus mechanism proof

Q3 establishes that the opportunity deserves consideration. Q8 later explains how the mechanism produces the result. Initial proof can include a documented case, a chart, a relevant third-party statement, a testimonial, or a demonstration. Choose evidence for the precise claim at hand.

Do not delay all substantiation until after a long pitch. The stronger the opening promise, the more quickly the reader needs something concrete.

### Proof is copy

Maintain relevance and readability during evidence sections. Explain what the evidence shows and why the reader should care. A chart without an interpretation asks the reader to do unnecessary work; an interpretation beyond the chart's scope overclaims.

## Key concepts

- **ABT:** background, conflict, and consequence expressed through And/But/Therefore.
- **Initial proof:** evidence that earns the next increment of attention.
- **Claim-proof fit:** whether the evidence actually supports the proposition beside it.
- **Narrative tension:** an unresolved development that makes the outcome matter.
- **Third-party proof:** evidence originating outside the seller, with relevance and context preserved.
- **Hypothetical result:** a reconstructed outcome that must not be presented as an actual customer experience.

## Mental models

- Treat every bold promise as creating an evidence obligation.
- Distinguish making proof easier to understand from making it stronger.
- Use a story to carry facts rather than replace them.

## Worked examples

### Trading indicator

The book reconstructs a company with poor earnings and sell ratings whose shares were falling. An indicator unexpectedly signals a buy, nothing initially happens, and the stock then rises after the market opens. The example ends by translating the move into a hypothetical trade outcome.

| ABT element | Reconstructed role |
|---|---|
| And | Poor results and declining shares create the expected direction |
| But | The indicator gives an unexpected signal, followed by uncertainty |
| Therefore | The later move illustrates the claimed informational advantage |

The source's hypothetical profit is not a verified investor result. A new application needs the actual signal time, entry/exit assumptions, losses, costs, and evidence relevant to its claim.

### Congressional Checks

Evaldo combines third-party descriptions of a tax provision with a contrast between informed lawmakers and unaware taxpayers. Disclosure documents are teased as the proof object. The analytical lesson is to organize quoted evidence around a relevant tension. It is not evidence that an invented program name is an official government entitlement.

### Scientific discovery

The book includes a Paul Amos health promotion built around cellular fuel and cancer. That is another copywriter's example, not Evaldo's authored medical research. Its narrative sequence illustrates discovery, contrast, and implication. Do not reuse its medical claims without appropriate independent support.

## Source rationale and limits

The chapter reports a charitable-giving study comparing a child's story, statistics, and a combination, with donation averages of $2.38, $1.14, and $1.43 respectively. Preserve that as the author's reported rationale. It does not justify a universal claim that facts reduce sales or that prospects should be kept from analytical thinking.

The Pontiac advertisement reproduced on p. 39 uses absurd desirability claims and a dramatic price contrast. Its job is to introduce promise inflation. It is not a performance benchmark.

## Anti-patterns

- **Evidence pile:** testimonials and facts accumulate without advancing the argument.
- **Authority mismatch:** a quote about a category is used to validate this particular offer.
- **Cherry-picked winners:** selected outcomes imply a reliability the full record may not show.
- **Narrative overreach:** sequence is mistaken for causation.
- **Exaggeration race:** a bigger promise creates skepticism the available proof cannot resolve.

## Key takeaways

1. Place initial proof close to the opening promise.
2. Make the proof's relevance explicit.
3. Use ABT to organize genuine evidence.
4. Keep actual, hypothetical, and forecast outcomes distinct.
5. Save the deeper mechanism explanation for Q8 without postponing credibility.
