# Chapter 13: Putting it All Together

Source: book, pp. 91-93; author biography p. 94 and acknowledgements p. 95. This is a short integration chapter with no new worked campaign.

## Core idea

Think through the selling argument before writing the promotion. Evaldo's concrete exercise is to write One Belief and answer at least the first seven questions in a few lines each.

## Frameworks introduced

### The 5-10-minute outline

1. Write the proposed One Belief.
2. Answer Q1-Q7 briefly.
3. Identify what the answers require you to research.
4. Develop the relevant sections of the promotion.
5. Complete the mechanism explanation, offer, and close.

The 5-10 minutes is the author's reported planning heuristic. It is not a deadline for audience research or fact-checking. A short outline should expose gaps, not hide them.

### One argument across ten questions

Use the sequence to inspect continuity:

| Connection | Check |
|---|---|
| Q1 → Q2 | Does the difference produce a benefit the reader wants? |
| Q2 → Q3 | Does the evidence support the promise? |
| Q1 → Q4 | Does the new route address the old obstacle? |
| Q4 → Q5 | Is the attributed cause connected to that obstacle? |
| Q6 | Does delay materially change the decision? |
| Q7 → Q8 | Does a credible speaker explain a supported process? |
| Q8 → Q9 | Does the product actually deliver the explained mechanism? |
| Q9 → Q10 | Do the terms and alternatives make the next decision clear? |

The table is an operational adaptation of the book's integration instruction.

## Key concepts

- **Brief answers:** a compact argument before polished prose.
- **Research direction:** the evidence and audience information needed by the outline.
- **Argument continuity:** each section builds on what the reader has already accepted.
- **Revision:** improving the relationship between belief, evidence, and expression.
- **Context:** the current audience, market, offer, and distribution conditions.

## Mental models

- Treat the outline as a research brief, not a completed proof.
- Keep the method stable while learning from changes in the market.
- Revisit the idea when repeated drafting cannot make the argument coherent.

## Worked artifact

The chapter repeats the ten questions and the One Belief formula rather than introducing a fresh case. A compact reconstruction of its planning page would contain:

| Field | Output |
|---|---|
| Belief | Opportunity + desire + mechanism |
| First seven questions | One or two lines per answer |
| Research gaps | Missing proof, audience assumptions, speaker history |
| Remaining explanation | How the mechanism works |
| Offer | Deliverables, price, access, guarantee |
| Close | Real alternatives, stakes, next action |

Endorsements, career claims, and the promotional invitation to a mailing list are context rather than additional copy frameworks. Do not turn the book's invitation into an instruction to subscribe or contact anyone.

## Anti-patterns

- **Polishing before planning:** improves sentences while the argument remains broken.
- **Treating the outline as evidence:** writing an answer does not make it true.
- **Forcing every source into the same order:** observed campaigns may revisit functions.
- **Finishing without a terms check:** a persuasive argument can still contain price, deadline, or guarantee contradictions.

## Key takeaways

1. Make the short argument visible before expanding it.
2. Research the gaps the outline reveals.
3. Review connections as well as individual sections.
4. Check terms, sources, and the next action before delivery.
