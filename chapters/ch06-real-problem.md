# Chapter 6: It's Time to Reveal the REAL Problem with Question #4

Source: book, pp. 48-53. Q4: "What's holding me back?"

## Core idea

Give the reader a credible explanation for why previous approaches did not solve the problem and why the proposed mechanism addresses that obstacle. This is the bridge from disappointment to a reason to try again.

## Frameworks introduced

### Reverse-engineer Q1 to find the real-problem hypothesis

Use when the audience has already tried related solutions and expects another disappointment.

1. Write the meaningful difference established by Q1.
2. Identify the limitation that difference is meant to overcome.
3. Check whether that limitation actually affected the prospect's previous attempts.
4. Explain why the previous approach left the limitation unresolved.
5. Show how the mechanism changes the relevant condition.
6. Support that explanation with logic and facts.

The final step is explicit in the book. Reverse-engineering is a way to generate an explanation, not a method of discovering truth without evidence.

### Justify past failure without humiliating the prospect

Evaldo draws on Blair Warren's work to argue that blame reduces receptivity. The copy should explain the missing factor instead of telling the reader they are lazy, foolish, or incapable.

Why it helps: a changed causal explanation can make a new attempt seem reasonable. Failure mode: declaring that everything was someone else's fault when the facts do not support that claim. Preserve agency and avoid giving the reader a false diagnosis.

### Hope through a changed mechanism

The book describes the emotional consequence as hope. The supplied lecture later names it the **magic conversion formula: real problem + new mechanism = hope**. Keep the naming attributed to the lecture while connecting the underlying reasoning to this chapter.

Hope requires more than reassurance. There must be a plausible difference between the old attempt and the proposed next attempt.

## Key concepts

- **Real problem:** the proposed cause of the reader's stalled progress.
- **Prior failure:** the result of an earlier attempt, which needs context rather than assumption.
- **Reverse-engineering:** using the new opportunity's contrast to form an obstacle hypothesis.
- **Justification:** an explanation that makes the past intelligible without personal attack.
- **Hope:** the expectation that a materially different route could change the outcome.
- **Mechanism-problem fit:** whether the offered process addresses the identified obstacle.

## Mental models

- Ask what will be different this time, not merely why this product sounds attractive.
- Treat the explanation of past failure as part of the current offer's causal argument.
- Use the reader's actual attempts as the starting point; do not invent a history for them.

## Worked example

In the Weekly Paycheck Indicator promotion, Q1 claims access to a signal before news becomes public. Evaldo reverses the timing contrast for Q4:

| Component | Source reasoning, reconstructed |
|---|---|
| Previous approach | Trading systems based on observed price movement |
| Proposed obstacle | By the time the signal appears, part of the move has happened |
| Consequence | The investor enters too late to capture the earlier opportunity |
| New route | Detect relevant activity before public news |
| Mechanism | The advertised indicator |

The reasoning unifies the opening and the problem section. It does not prove that all competing systems work as described or that a before-news signal is reliable. Those remain separate evidence questions.

The book also gives hypothetical weight-loss explanations involving hormones, thyroid function, or gut bacteria. Their role is to illustrate a changed explanation. Do not convert them into diagnoses for an individual or presume that a supplement solves the reader's problem.

## Application artifact

For a new brief, record these four sentences before drafting:

1. "The audience has tried [documented approach]."
2. "That approach can leave [supported limitation] unresolved because [reason]."
3. "Our mechanism changes [specific condition] through [process]."
4. "The evidence for that connection is [source or evidence gap]."

This is an operational adaptation. If sentence four is empty, research or narrow the claim before turning it into confident copy.

## Anti-patterns

- **Blaming the reader:** may make the letter feel hostile rather than helpful.
- **Universal absolution:** removes real conditions or responsibilities from the decision.
- **Convenient invented cause:** fits the product but not the evidence.
- **Unrelated novelty:** the new feature does not address the old failure.
- **Repeating "this time is different":** promises a change without explaining it.

## Key takeaways

1. Connect the proposed obstacle directly to the opening distinction.
2. Check the causal story rather than treating a reversed sentence as proof.
3. Explain how the mechanism changes the relevant condition.
4. Use hope as the consequence of a credible new route.
