# Chapter 12: It's Time to Close with the Powerful Push-Pull Technique

Source: book, pp. 84-90. Q10: "What do I have to lose?"

## Core idea

Finish by clarifying the reader's options and the stakes of the decision without sounding desperate for the purchase. The close should resolve the argument rather than compensate for an unproven belief.

## Frameworks introduced

### Push-pull

1. **Push:** acknowledge that the offer may not be appropriate and that the decision belongs to the reader.
2. **Pull:** reconnect the opportunity to the desired outcome and the supported cost of remaining in the current situation.
3. Explain the next action and its terms.
4. Leave the reader a genuine choice.

The book describes creating the impression of disinterest and an illusion of control, drawing an analogy from pickup-artist tactics. Preserve that as source analysis; apply the useful commercial function by respecting real choice rather than manufacturing rejection or insulting the buyer.

Why it may help: excessive pressure can trigger reactance. Failure mode: faux indifference or aggressive disqualification can make the speaker sound manipulative rather than confident.

### Three-option close

The source's alternatives are:

- Do nothing.
- Do it yourself.
- Let the provider do the heavy lifting.

Use when these reflect real alternatives. Explain the effort, benefit, and trade-off of each. Do not pretend they exhaust the market if other meaningful routes exist.

### Return to One Belief

Remind the reader of the connection between opportunity, desire, and mechanism. If that connection remains weak, a harder close will not repair it. Fix the earlier section instead.

## Key concepts

- **Push:** creating distance or acknowledging non-fit.
- **Pull:** returning attention to the desired outcome and unresolved problem.
- **Neediness:** language that communicates the seller's desperation rather than the buyer's interest.
- **Cost of inaction:** a supported consequence of leaving the problem unresolved.
- **Three-option close:** a comparison of staying put, acting independently, and accepting help.
- **Decision ownership:** the reader's ability to evaluate and decline the offer.

## Mental models

- Treat the close as decision support after the selling argument, not a final burst of pressure.
- Distinguish what the customer risks by buying from what they give up by waiting.
- Use a clear next action to resolve procedural doubt without recreating the entire pitch.

## Worked example

The book's weekend-trading promotion recaps claimed gains, a discounted charter membership, and a performance guarantee before presenting three options.

| Option | How the source frames it | What to preserve in an adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | May fit someone already financially comfortable | A genuine non-fit condition |
| Do it yourself | Track news and analyze a large stock universe | A fair account of the work required |
| Accept help | Receive filtered research and alerts | The actual service and buyer responsibilities |

The source then contrasts dreamers with people who act and refers to a limited group of 250 buyers. Those devices are part of the observed copy. Do not reproduce a capacity limit without evidence or equate declining the offer with personal failure.

A faithful structural reconstruction stops short of copying the historical profit promises. The reader should be able to compare effort and access without assuming that assistance guarantees the desired financial outcome.

## Practical revision procedure

Read only the last section and mark each sentence by job: recap, terms, choice, next step, or pressure. Remove pressure that adds no decision-relevant information. Verify that the proposed action is clear and that any reassurance matches what actually happens next.

For example, the Eccentric Millionaire button says the order can be reviewed before becoming final. That addresses a concrete fear of an immediate charge. Use such reassurance only when the actual flow supports it.

This procedure is an adaptation for applying the chapter, not a separately named method from the book.

## Anti-patterns

- **Begging:** repeats purchase requests without resolving another concern.
- **Shaming:** implies a refusal proves the reader is unserious or inadequate.
- **Artificial non-fit:** pretends to reject the reader merely to increase pursuit.
- **Unfair alternatives:** exaggerates DIY difficulty or omits relevant competing options.
- **Late unsupported promise:** introduces a new claim when the reader is deciding.
- **Vague CTA:** creates uncertainty about the immediate next step.

## Key takeaways

1. Recap the belief and terms without escalating the promise.
2. Give genuine room to decline.
3. Compare alternatives fairly.
4. Make the next action and its consequences clear.
5. Repair missing proof earlier rather than applying more closing pressure.
