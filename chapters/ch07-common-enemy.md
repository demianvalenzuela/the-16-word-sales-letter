# Chapter 7: It's Time to Play the "Us vs. Them" Card with Question #5

Source: book, pp. 54-60. Q5: "Who/What is to blame?"

## Core idea

Identify a cause or shared obstacle the reader already recognizes, and explain how the offer helps them respond. The distinction between Q4 and Q5 matters: Q4 describes what blocks progress; Q5 attributes the cause or opposing force.

## Frameworks introduced

### Us versus them

Use when a credible shared frustration helps explain the problem and the proposed route. Evaldo argues that shared opposition can create group identification.

1. Research what the audience already distrusts or finds frustrating.
2. Identify the specific behavior, incentive, practice, or constraint involved.
3. Check its factual connection to the obstacle in Q4.
4. Explain the common interest between speaker and reader.
5. Return to how the mechanism helps the reader act.

The book explicitly advises against inventing an enemy out of nowhere. Existing beliefs are research inputs, not automatic evidence that the blamed party caused the problem.

### Borrow existing beliefs

The chapter's historical newsletter audience is described as conservative and distrustful of Wall Street, media, and political opponents. Health promotions use pharmaceutical pricing as a shared grievance. These are audience-specific examples, not universal customer profiles.

Why the author uses this: an accepted concern can make the speaker feel aligned with the reader. Failure mode: importing a grievance from another audience, intensifying an unsupported accusation, or letting anger displace the selling argument.

## Key concepts

- **Common enemy:** a person, institution, practice, or situation presented as a shared obstacle.
- **Group identification:** the feeling that the speaker and reader belong on the same side of a problem.
- **Existing belief:** a belief the prospect brings to the message.
- **Blame attribution:** the explanation of who or what contributes to the obstacle.
- **Shared interest:** the reason the speaker's proposed action benefits the reader.

## Mental models

- Prefer a concrete mechanism of harm to a vague villain label.
- Keep the product relevant: if removing the enemy paragraph leaves the mechanism unchanged, the paragraph may be emotional decoration.
- Distinguish "the reader believes this" from "the available evidence establishes this."

## Worked examples

### Information asymmetry on Wall Street

Evaldo's copy contrasts everyday investors on the outside with insiders who get valuable information earlier. This connects Q4's late entry to Q5's unequal access. The proposed indicator is then framed as a way to reduce that disadvantage.

The argument can be reconstructed as:

| Step | Role |
|---|---|
| Identify late information | States the obstacle |
| Attribute it to unequal access | Explains a perceived structural disadvantage |
| Align with ordinary investors | Establishes shared interest |
| Present a changed route | Connects the emotion to the mechanism |

The lesson is causal continuity. A new application still needs support for claims about access, competing methods, and the offered advantage.

### Apple comparisons

The chapter cites Apple's 1984 advertisement and Mac-versus-PC ads as examples of making an alternative stand for a disliked experience. The supplied lecture also plays Mac/PC commercials. These are external illustrations; the corpus does not attribute their authorship to Evaldo or provide conversion data.

## Source material consciously bounded

The chapter discusses cults, racial group responses, war, and Evaldo's experience as an international student after September 11. It uses these to argue that group categorization can happen quickly. Preserve that rationale in study; do not treat harmful historical examples as commercial operating instructions. The source's claims about oxytocin and a "reptilian brain" are not independently established by this skill.

For an ordinary business application, the shared obstacle may be a fragmented workflow, a costly incentive, or a conventional practice with documented drawbacks. That is an adaptation of Q5, not a named example supplied by Evaldo.

## Anti-patterns

- **Manufacturing an enemy:** produces an unsupported explanation.
- **Importing politics:** assumes the historical newsletter audience matches the present one.
- **Blame with no causal link:** creates emotion unrelated to the offer.
- **Demonizing the reader's past choice:** can undermine rapport.
- **Opposition without a route forward:** leaves the reader angry but no closer to a decision.

## Key takeaways

1. Separate the obstacle from the attributed cause.
2. Use actual audience research rather than a stereotype.
3. Ground blame in a specific, supportable connection.
4. Return from shared frustration to the mechanism and desired outcome.
