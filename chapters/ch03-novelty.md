# Chapter 3: Injecting Dopamine into Your Prospect's Brain with Question #1

Source: book, pp. 29-33. Q1: "How is this different from everything else I've seen?"

## Core idea

Give the reader a concrete reason to see the offer as a new possibility before asking for sustained attention. The difference should matter to the selling belief rather than function as an unrelated surprise.

## Frameworks introduced

### Lead with the new opportunity

Use when a market has heard the same promise repeatedly or a draft opens with a long introduction.

1. List the approaches the reader is likely to recognize.
2. Identify what the current opportunity changes: timing, access, method, constraint, discovery, or another supported distinction.
3. Put that difference in the headline or opening.
4. Give it a concrete form where possible, such as a demonstration, document, or observable contrast.
5. Connect the distinction promptly to the reader's benefit in Q2.

The purpose is to make further reading worthwhile. "New" by itself is weak because it makes no distinction the reader can inspect.

### Discovery framing

Evaldo wants the opening to feel like revealing something newly understood. That can be a law, a threat, a loophole, a prediction, or a method in his historical financial examples. Select the frame that describes the actual material. Do not convert routine information into a fictitious discovery.

Why it may help: an unresolved but relevant difference gives the reader a reason to investigate. Failure mode: mystery without enough substance creates confusion or distrust.

## Key concepts

- **Novelty:** a perceived departure from familiar information or approaches.
- **New opportunity:** the distinctive route within One Belief.
- **Discovery frame:** a presentation that reveals a newly recognized possibility.
- **Concrete evidence object:** a visible artifact that makes a claim inspectable.
- **Pattern interrupt:** the introduction's related idea of an immediately recognizable or unexpected opening.
- **Attention hypothesis:** the claim that a particular distinction will earn further reading; it still needs testing in the current audience.

## Mental models

- Ask what the reader can explain as different after the first few lines.
- Use a contrast the prospect already understands to make unfamiliar information intelligible.
- Treat novelty as an entrance to the argument. It cannot substitute for benefit or proof.

## Worked example

In the Weekly Paycheck Indicator opening, the speaker briefly names himself and postpones a full introduction. He holds a patent application and describes an indicator intended to identify market-moving activity before news becomes public.

The sequence, reconstructed:

| Move | Function |
|---|---|
| Brief identification | Establishes a speaker without a long biography |
| Physical document | Makes the opening specific and visible |
| Claimed new indicator | Introduces the mechanism as a distinct object |
| Before-news contrast | Explains the proposed advantage over reacting after publication |

The later lecture shows the speaker reading Evaldo's script from a teleprompter. The copywriter and the person saying "I" are different people. Keep that distinction when adapting examples to a client.

The patent reference is useful to study as a novelty cue. It does not prove that a patent was granted, that the underlying performance claim is correct, or that the mechanism is exclusive. The source's identification number should not appear in a new client's copy unless it belongs to that client and has been checked.

## Source claims and limits

The chapter invokes a novelty study by Nico Bunzeck and Emrah Düzel, dopamine, and ideas from Oren Klaff. It also recounts Einstein receiving a compass. These supply the author's rationale and analogy. The operational rule here is to make the distinction salient; do not promise that a sentence reliably produces a measurable neurochemical response. The corpus does not establish such a copy-level measurement.

## Anti-patterns

- **Opening with credentials alone:** delays the reason the reader should care.
- **Calling a familiar category revolutionary:** relies on an adjective instead of a difference.
- **Novelty unrelated to the offer:** earns attention that the following argument cannot use.
- **Giving the entire technical explanation immediately:** can obscure the simple contrast the opening needs.
- **Using a document as proof of everything:** confuses evidence of one fact with evidence of the full promise.

## Key takeaways

1. State the specific distinction early.
2. Give the distinction a tangible form when the evidence allows it.
3. Follow novelty with benefit and initial proof.
4. Match the claim to what the evidence object establishes.
