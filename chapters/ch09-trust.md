# Chapter 9: How to Make Your Prospect Know, Trust, and Like You

Source: book, pp. 66-73. Q7: "Why should I trust you?"

## Core idea

Establish why this speaker understands the problem, can help, and has a credible reason to share the solution. Use a relevant story rather than a detached resume.

## Frameworks introduced

### Three credibility storylines

| Storyline | Use when | Build the story around |
|---|---|---|
| I've been in your shoes | The speaker genuinely experienced a comparable struggle | Shared problem → failed attempts or low point → discovery → change |
| Robin Hood | Real insider experience can be made useful to an underserved audience | Insider access → dissatisfaction with the old arrangement → decision to share |
| Expert | Relevant experience and achievements establish competence | Work performed → specific expertise → evidence of capability |

The book calls the first storyline the strongest and permits combining the three. Do not manufacture the stronger story when only the expert story is supported.

### Credibility through relevance

1. Select the reader's actual reason for doubt.
2. Identify the part of the speaker's history that addresses it.
3. Tell only the events needed to establish the relevant connection.
4. Support achievements and results with appropriate evidence.
5. Return from biography to what the reader can do with the help offered.

Why it helps: the reader sees more than a credential list; they understand why the speaker is relevant to this problem. Failure mode: the story creates affinity while leaving actual competence unproven.

### Emotional sale before fuller explanation

Evaldo reports answering the first seven questions in roughly the first third of his letters. He treats these as establishing the emotional case, followed by Q8's logical explanation. This is his planning heuristic, not a fixed layout requirement or a universal theory of buying behavior.

## Key concepts

- **Credibility:** a reason to accept the speaker's competence and account.
- **Relatability:** recognizable experience shared with the audience.
- **Authority:** relevant expertise or position, demonstrated rather than merely signaled.
- **Motive:** the reason the speaker is making the offer or sharing information.
- **Robin Hood storyline:** making insider knowledge accessible to outsiders.
- **Borrowed authority:** recognition from others, whose relevance still needs examination.

## Mental models

- Ask why the reader should listen to this speaker about this issue.
- Treat biography as evidence for a specific concern, not a chronological obligation.
- Use the speaker's genuine vulnerability when it explains their route; do not add a low point simply to create drama.

## Worked examples

### I've been in your shoes

The book's penny-stock example starts with the speaker frustrated by slow-moving conventional stocks, describes a middle-class background, and explains a switch to another strategy. It then introduces a student's result to suggest that the route is not restricted to the speaker.

The structure has two distinct evidence burdens: the speaker's story and the student's actual result. Neither automatically proves what a new reader can expect. Preserve that separation in an adaptation.

### Robin Hood

A derivatives trader describes working with large accounts, earning substantial money, and enjoying an affluent lifestyle. Dissatisfaction with helping the already wealthy motivates leaving Wall Street and sharing an income secret. The story connects access, disillusionment, and motive.

Transfer the sequence only when the history is real. An invented insider secret undermines the very trust the section is meant to build.

### Expert

Another example follows a trader from a 1991 start at the Chicago Board of Trade through progressively responsible roles and later media appearances. The valuable detail is the relevant experience accumulated, not a pile of prestigious names.

## Source analogy and limit

The chapter opens with Frank Abagnale obtaining a pilot's uniform, using it to illustrate authority signals. The lesson for analysis is that a signal can produce trust without proving capability. Generated commercial copy should use legitimate evidence, not deceptive impersonation. The anecdote itself is not independently verified by this skill.

## Anti-patterns

- **Resume dump:** achievements appear without connection to the reader's doubt.
- **Invented adversity:** adds a fabricated transformation to imitate the preferred storyline.
- **Celebrity proximity as competence:** a photograph proves proximity at most.
- **Audience mismatch:** the speaker's journey does not resemble the reader's situation.
- **Guaranteeing replication:** assumes the reader will reproduce an exceptional result.

## Key takeaways

1. Choose the strongest truthful storyline.
2. Connect each biographical detail to a reason to trust.
3. Combine storylines when the facts support the combination.
4. Distinguish affinity, expertise, motive, and performance evidence.
