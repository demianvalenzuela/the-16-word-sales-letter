# Chapter 4: The Man Who Helped Ending World War I with Toilet Paper

Source: book, pp. 34-38. Q2: "What's in it for me?"

## Core idea

Translate the new opportunity into something the reader already wants, and do so before the reader has to work to infer it. Evaldo's default is to answer Q1 and Q2 on the first page.

## Frameworks introduced

### Reader-interest framing

Use when a draft explains the product, company, or discovery but leaves the buyer's reason to care implicit.

1. Identify the reader's immediate problem and the desired change.
2. Translate the relevant feature into a benefit for that reader.
3. Make the outcome specific enough to understand.
4. Add only the conditions or qualifiers needed to state it accurately.
5. Put that promise near the opening distinction.

The chapter connects this to Dale Carnegie's focus on other people's interests. The word "you" is a useful orientation, but merely replacing "we" with "you" does not create a meaningful benefit.

### Specific promise tied to a core desire

The promise should connect to the desire inside One Belief. That could be security in retirement, supporting a family, physical wellbeing, freedom, or another researched outcome. The book's extreme health and income promises illustrate its direct-response context, not a permission to invent results.

Why it helps: the reader can quickly decide whether the opportunity is relevant. Failure mode: making the promise more dramatic than the supporting evidence can bear increases disbelief and weakens Q3.

## Key concepts

- **Benefit:** a change that matters to the reader.
- **Big promise:** the central outcome the promotion offers to help produce.
- **Core desire:** the motivation behind the stated outcome.
- **Reader relevance:** the connection between the topic and the reader's situation.
- **First-page default:** Evaldo's placement preference for novelty and benefit, not a universal word count.

## Mental models

- Treat attention as a choice the reader keeps making, not a resource the writer owns.
- Imagine the reader returning to another activity if the opening cannot answer why this matters.
- Keep the product's features as reasons the benefit is possible rather than making the feature inventory the opening argument.

## Worked example

The chapter tells a historical story about Harry Reichenbach and leaflets intended to persuade German soldiers to surrender. In the author's telling, research with prisoners identified concrete unmet needs: food, clothing, personal supplies, and treatment. The offer translated surrender into specific improvements the soldier could understand, including daily provisions and officer rank.

The transferable reasoning is:

| Research observation | Copy decision |
|---|---|
| The audience faces immediate deprivation | Address concrete needs rather than abstract institutional aims |
| The desired action appears costly or threatening | Explain what changes for the person taking it |
| Generic reassurance may not persuade | State specific provisions and terms |

This reconstructs the book's example. The historical account has not been independently verified here, and the wartime context is not a model for commercial pressure.

The chapter also illustrates the principle with a parent linking homework to a desired later activity. In Evaldo's own financial copy, the Weekly Paycheck Indicator's claimed before-news advantage is followed by a specific weekly-income promise. The useful sequence is difference → desired outcome, not the financial amount itself.

## How to diagnose a weak Q2

Remove the product name and read the opening. Can a prospect still describe what improves for them? If not, the draft may be asking the audience to perform the translation. If several outcomes compete, choose the one most directly connected to the selling belief and keep the others subordinate.

If the strongest supported benefit is narrower than the headline, fix the headline. Do not use a vague qualifier to preserve an otherwise unsupported number.

## Anti-patterns

- **Long preamble:** the reader never reaches the benefit.
- **Feature masquerading as benefit:** a proprietary system or report length does not explain the reader's gain.
- **Generic better-life promise:** lacks a concrete connection to the current problem.
- **Escalating promises without evidence:** makes the next section's credibility burden unmanageable.
- **Treating an attention-span number as a law:** the book itself questions the precision of the popular eight-second claim.

## Key takeaways

1. Answer why the reader should care as soon as the difference is clear.
2. Ground the promise in a desire rather than a feature list.
3. Use specifics that can be supported.
4. Make Q3 the next natural step: why should the reader believe the promise?
