# Patterns and procedures

Source labels distinguish book teaching, direct lecture, observed example, and third-party interpretation. Adaptations below operationalize the source rather than claim a new Evaldo framework.

## One Belief before prose

**When to use:** The idea, mission, or outline is unclear.

**How:** Separate opportunity, desire, and mechanism. Write their relationship in one sentence. Check the causal and competitive claims it implies. Outline Q1-Q7 briefly before expanding.

**Trade-offs:** Focus helps reject digressions; an oversimplified belief can hide multiple audiences or unsupported exclusivity.

**Source:** Book ch02 and ch13.

## Novelty → benefit → initial proof

**When to use:** The opening delays relevance or asks for trust too soon.

**How:** State the specific difference, connect it to the desired result, and provide relevant evidence. The book's placement default is Q1-Q2 on the first page, with proof following promptly.

**Trade-offs:** Early specificity aids comprehension but increases the evidence burden. Avoid overwhelming the opening with the full mechanism.

**Source:** Book ch03-ch05.

## ABT evidence story

**When to use:** A proof section reads like a list of disconnected facts.

**How:** Establish conditions, introduce a contradiction or obstacle, then show the documented action/result and its significance. The causal relationship matters more than literal connective words.

**Trade-offs:** A story makes evidence easier to process; it can also conceal selection bias if context is removed.

**Source:** Book ch05 and ch10, drawing on Olson/Parker.

## Reverse the difference into the obstacle

**When to use:** Prospects have tried similar solutions and expect another failure.

**How:** Turn the Q1 contrast into a Q4 hypothesis. Check it against prior attempts. Explain how the new mechanism changes that condition. Use facts to support the connection.

**Trade-offs:** Creates a coherent reason to try again; becomes circular if the obstacle is invented merely to fit the offer.

**Source:** Book ch06; lecture's real problem + new mechanism = hope.

## Shared obstacle with causal continuity

**When to use:** A recognized frustration helps explain the reader's situation.

**How:** Identify an existing concern, establish its connection to Q4, show the shared interest, and return to the proposed action.

**Trade-offs:** Can create alignment; imported politics or unsupported blame can damage credibility.

**Source:** Book ch07. Using a documented workflow or incentive as the obstacle is an application adaptation.

## Event → change → consequence of delay

**When to use:** The reader agrees but sees no reason to act now.

**How:** Identify what actually happens, what changes afterward, and how that affects the buyer's goal. Preserve the difference between a confirmed deadline and a possible event outcome.

**Trade-offs:** Real urgency aids prioritization. Forecast certainty and false lifetime scarcity undermine trust.

**Source:** Book ch08, bounded application of its urgency teaching.

## Select a truthful credibility story

**When to use:** The reader doubts the speaker's competence, understanding, or motive.

**How:** Choose shared experience, legitimate insider access, or expertise based on actual history. Include only details that answer the doubt. Combine stories when supported.

**Trade-offs:** A story can create affinity without proving the mechanism; attach the right evidence.

**Source:** Book ch09.

## Mechanism without its branded name

**When to use:** A named system remains a black box.

**How:** Explain the input, process, relevant change, and desired outcome. Use evidence at the important links, a familiar starting point, and ABT where helpful.

**Trade-offs:** Enough explanation supports the decision; unnecessary technical detail obscures it.

**Source:** Book ch10; lecture's unusual-options-activity clarification.

## Complementary bonus design

**When to use:** The offer seems thin or the buyer may struggle to use the product.

**How:** Identify an implementation obstacle and create a real deliverable addressing it. Explain that job before assigning a defensible value. Recap components and check the total.

**Trade-offs:** Useful additions increase value; unrelated extras add cognitive load and support burden.

**Source:** Book ch11, Todd Brown's cited premiums principle, observed crypto package.

## Report-led subscription offer

**When to use:** A specific information package leads naturally into ongoing research.

**How:** Introduce report, service, testimonials, high anchor, bonuses, stack, price, and guarantee. Make the required payment and delivery terms explicit.

**Trade-offs:** The report can be more desirable than a generic subscription; unclear conditions can make "free" misleading.

**Source:** Book ch11 and Eccentric Millionaire pp. 27-37.

## Push-pull and real alternatives

**When to use:** A close sounds needy or excessively forceful.

**How:** Acknowledge non-fit, state the supported stakes, compare real options, and explain the next step. Use do nothing/DIY/help when appropriate.

**Trade-offs:** Choice reduces pressure; contrived rejection and unfair alternatives defeat the purpose.

**Source:** Book ch12, with explicit separation from its illusion-of-control rhetoric.

## Claim → concrete implication → proof loop

**When to use:** A number or abstract benefit is hard to imagine.

**How:** State the supported claim, translate it into a familiar unit or situation, then show evidence with relevant context. Repeat only when the next loop advances a different concern.

**Trade-offs:** Repetition can strengthen understanding or become redundant. Hypotheticals must remain distinct from actual results.

**Source:** Observed promotion and third-party interpretation, not a named book framework.

## Open question with a paid-off explanation

**When to use:** A longer explanation benefits from a clear reason to continue.

**How:** Introduce a meaningful unanswered question, indicate what the reader will learn, and deliver the explanation. Track each loop to its resolution.

**Trade-offs:** Curiosity can sustain reading; repeated delay without substance damages trust.

**Source:** Observed promotion and third-party breakdown.

## Match voice and reduce next-click uncertainty

**When to use:** Copy feels unlike the client or the CTA leaves uncertainty.

**How:** Derive voice cues from the client's material, retain truthful biography, and describe what the next action actually does. Place relevant reassurance near that action.

**Trade-offs:** Familiarity supports continuity; adopting another person's life story or claiming an unverified checkout behavior is misleading.

**Source:** Third-party commentary and observed CTA. These are supplementary application patterns.
