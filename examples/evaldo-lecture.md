## Listerine: positioning exercise

Evaldo discusses an early mouthwash campaign using the term halitosis and the desire for social acceptance. His reconstruction links eliminating halitosis to avoiding social rejection through a germ-killing mouthwash.

| Layer | Role in his explanation |
|---|---|
| Product function | Address bad breath |
| Opportunity framing | A named condition that feels more specific |
| Desire | Acceptance and avoiding social embarrassment |
| Mechanism | Antiseptic/germ-killing action |

The transferable decision is to connect a functional benefit to a meaningful desire. Renaming an ordinary problem does not establish a new medical condition or new efficacy. His historical account remains a source claim.

This advertisement is an external example discussed by Evaldo, not a campaign he wrote.

## Whiteboard before prose

The lecture describes meetings with writers before drafting. One Belief appears at the top of the whiteboard, with the ten questions underneath. The practical benefit is that the team knows what to write and what to research next.

Apply this as a short shared argument, not a requirement to create a literal whiteboard. If the opportunity cannot be explained clearly, investigate the idea before expanding it.

## Magic conversion formula

**Real problem + new mechanism = hope.**

The lecture uses a hypothetical weight-loss explanation and P90X's plateau/muscle-confusion story. It then reverses the Weekly Paycheck Indicator's before-news distinction into the after-news obstacle.

Use the formula when a prospect has reasons to expect another failure. The mechanism must change the factor that made the old route fail. A convenient diagnosis is not enough, and a supplement example should not become individual health advice.

The book teaches this relationship in ch06 without presenting that exact named formula. Attribute the name to this lecture.

## What is behind the Weekly Paycheck Indicator

Evaldo explains that the advertised indicator is based on unusual options activity. The narrative premise is that some trades may precede public information. The copy later explains this so the branded mechanism does not remain a mystery.

This helps separate three things:

1. **Reader-facing name:** Weekly Paycheck Indicator.
2. **Process described:** observing unusual options activity.
3. **Promised outcome:** getting positioned before news and earning money.

The lecture supplies the process description, not a verified performance record. Future applications should not infer reliability from the mechanism's plausibility.

## Urgency and the expert's forecast

Evaldo recounts asking the spokesperson what events could create urgency and choosing a possible Amazon/Bitcoin announcement. He says the idea came from the expert rather than being invented independently by the copywriter.

This reveals the development process. It does not change a forecast into a confirmed event. Keep the same distinction when using a client's opinion in a new campaign.

## Credibility, explanation, and offer

The lecture reiterates the three credibility stories, identifying "I've been in your shoes" as the most powerful in his experience. It cites a Craig Clemens/Dr. Gundry promotion as an example of expertise combined with personal struggle. The transcription garbles the copywriter's name; do not quote the garble as an authoritative spelling.

It also reiterates that proof and mechanism explanation remain copy. The newsletter offer is report → service → testimonials → false close → bonuses → stack/price → guarantee. The closing alternatives are do nothing, do it yourself, or accept help.
